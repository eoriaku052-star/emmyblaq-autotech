TECHNICAL KNOWLEDGE • DIAGNOSTIC METHODS

Advanced Automotive Diagnostic Guides

Practical technical information on automotive electrical systems, fault-code interpretation, circuit testing and vehicle air-conditioning. Built for technicians, apprentices and serious vehicle owners.

How to use these guides: Confirm the customer's complaint, inspect the system, test the relevant circuit and compare your findings with manufacturer specifications. A fault code is a starting point for diagnosis—not automatic proof that a particular component has failed.

1. Advanced electrical fault diagnosis

Start with measurements and circuit logic. Replacing parts without confirming the cause can waste time and money.

Voltage-drop testing: finding resistance hidden in a circuit

A cable can look clean and still have excessive resistance inside a crimp, connector, fuse holder or ground joint. Continuity testing alone may not reveal the problem because a multimeter uses very little current during that test.

When is voltage-drop testing useful?

  • A starter clicks but cranks slowly.
  • A headlamp becomes dim under load.
  • A cooling fan runs intermittently.
  • A connector or cable becomes unusually warm.
  • A component works during a bench test but fails in the vehicle.

Diagnostic procedure

  1. Identify the correct circuit using the vehicle's wiring diagram.
  2. Inspect the battery, terminals, connectors, fuses and ground points.
  3. Set the multimeter to DC volts and select a suitable range.
  4. With the circuit operating under its normal load, measure across the section being tested.
  5. Compare the reading with the manufacturer's allowable voltage drop.
  6. If the drop is excessive, divide the circuit into smaller sections and retest to isolate the resistance.
Example: If a cable section shows a voltage drop that exceeds the vehicle manufacturer's specification while carrying its normal load, investigate the cable, crimps and connections in that section. Do not condemn the component until the complete circuit has been checked.

Positive-side and ground-side checks

On a suitable powered circuit, measure the positive supply path separately from the ground return path. An excessive drop on the positive side points towards resistance in the supply path; an excessive ground-side drop points towards the return path. Follow the correct test procedure for the circuit being diagnosed.

Common mistake: Measuring resistance on an energised circuit. Isolate power and follow the service procedure before using resistance or continuity mode.

2. OBD-II scanner interpretation

How to diagnose a fault code without guessing

An OBD-II diagnostic trouble code (DTC) records information about a detected system fault. It does not always identify the failed part. A sensor-related code, for example, can result from wiring damage, poor power supply, a bad ground, a connector problem or a genuine sensor failure.

A professional scan workflow

  1. Confirm the vehicle year, make, model, engine and system.
  2. Ask when the symptom occurs and whether any recent repairs were made.
  3. Scan the relevant modules and record all codes before clearing anything.
  4. Check code status, freeze-frame information and related codes when available.
  5. Compare live data with manufacturer specifications and the actual operating conditions.
  6. Use wiring diagrams and electrical tests to confirm the suspected cause.
  7. Repair the verified fault, clear codes where appropriate, and retest the vehicle.

Useful scanner information

Scanner data What it helps you investigate
Stored DTCs Faults recorded by a control module.
Pending DTCs Problems detected but not necessarily confirmed as a mature fault.
Freeze-frame data Operating conditions captured when a qualifying fault was recorded, if supported.
Live data Sensor and calculated values that can be compared with expected behaviour.
Readiness monitors Whether supported emissions-monitor tests have completed.

Example: a mass-airflow sensor code

A code relating to the mass-airflow (MAF) signal does not automatically mean the MAF sensor is defective. Inspect the air intake for leaks, check the connector and wiring, verify the sensor's power and ground where applicable, and compare its signal with the correct service information. Consider other engine conditions that may influence the reading.

Technician's rule: Save the original scan report. Clearing codes before recording them can remove useful diagnostic evidence. Never replace a part based only on the code description.

3. Wiring diagrams and circuit tracing

How to trace an automotive circuit from the diagram

A wiring diagram helps you understand how a component receives power, how its ground or control path works, and which connectors and protection devices lie between the source and the load.

What to identify before testing

  • Battery or ignition-switched power source.
  • Fuse and relay locations, ratings and circuit function.
  • Connector identification and terminal numbers.
  • Wire colours, splices and ground points.
  • Whether a control module switches the positive side or ground side.
  • Any shared supply or ground used by other components.

Example: an electrical cooling fan that will not run

  1. Confirm the reported fault and check the relevant fuse.
  2. Use the correct wiring diagram to identify the fan's power, ground and control arrangements.
  3. Determine whether the system is designed to activate under the current engine temperature or AC operating conditions.
  4. Inspect connectors and wiring, then test the supply and ground using the specified procedure.
  5. If a relay is fitted, verify its control signal and switched output instead of assuming the relay is bad.
  6. Use a suitable scan tool to check commanded fan operation if the vehicle supports it.
  7. Confirm the repair by retesting the system under the correct operating conditions.
Important: Some fans can start unexpectedly, even after the engine has been switched off. Keep hands and tools clear and follow the manufacturer's isolation procedure.

4. Automotive air-conditioning diagnosis

Car AC is not cooling: a systematic diagnostic approach

Poor cooling can result from airflow restrictions, condenser fan faults, incorrect refrigerant charge, compressor-control problems, blend-door faults or other issues. Pressure readings must be interpreted with the ambient temperature, humidity, engine speed and manufacturer test conditions in mind.

Begin with basic observations

  1. Confirm the complaint and note whether cooling is poor at idle, while driving, or in both conditions.
  2. Check the cabin blower, air filter and airflow from the vents.
  3. Check whether the condenser fan operates when commanded, using the correct test conditions.
  4. Inspect the compressor drive or electrical control system, depending on compressor design.
  5. Read relevant AC and engine control-module codes where supported.
  6. Measure system pressures with approved equipment and compare them with the vehicle manufacturer's specifications.
  7. After testing, repair the confirmed cause and verify vent performance under repeatable conditions.

Understanding common symptoms

Observed symptom Possible areas to investigate
Cooling improves while driving but weakens at idle Condenser airflow, fan operation and system performance under the specified test conditions.
Blower runs but air is warm Compressor command, refrigerant circuit, blend-door operation and system controls.
Compressor does not engage Control request, fuse, relay if fitted, wiring, pressure inputs and compressor-control strategy.
Cooling changes unpredictably Intermittent electrical connections, sensor inputs, fan control and system operating conditions.

These are diagnostic starting points, not definitive fault matches. Several different faults can produce the same symptom.

Refrigerant safety: Do not vent refrigerant to the atmosphere or open a pressurised AC circuit casually. Use suitable recovery equipment, correct protective equipment and the refrigerant specified for the vehicle. Never mix refrigerants.

5. Real workshop case studies

Case-study method: document the evidence, not just the repair

A useful workshop case study records the complaint, the checks performed, the measurements obtained, the confirmed cause and the result after repair. This helps another technician understand why the repair was justified.

Recommended case-study format

  1. Vehicle: Record the make, model, year and engine where known.
  2. Complaint: Describe exactly what the driver noticed.
  3. Initial inspection: Record visible damage, warning lights and scan results.
  4. Tests: Explain which circuit or system was tested and why.
  5. Evidence: Record actual measurements and compare them with the relevant specifications.
  6. Confirmed cause: State the fault only after the evidence supports it.
  7. Repair and verification: Explain what was repaired and how normal operation was confirmed.

Example: If an intermittent electrical fault is traced to a loose ground connection, document the symptoms, the measured voltage drop before repair, the connection repair and the repeat-test result. Do not publish invented readings or present an unverified diagnosis as a real customer repair.

6. Technical accuracy and safe working

Before carrying out any diagnostic test

  • Use wiring diagrams and service information for the exact vehicle.
  • Check the meter leads, probe ratings and measurement mode before testing.
  • Never measure resistance on a powered circuit.
  • Avoid probing airbag and other safety-critical circuits unless trained and following the approved procedure.
  • Use a suitable fused test lead when required; never short a power circuit to ground to “see what happens.”
  • Follow approved procedures for hybrid and electric vehicles. High-voltage systems require appropriate training and isolation.
  • Record findings so another technician can understand the diagnostic process.

Specifications and diagnostic strategies differ between manufacturers and models. These guides provide general educational information and are not a substitute for vehicle-specific service documentation or qualified training.
