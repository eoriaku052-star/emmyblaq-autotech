1. Voltage-Drop Testing and Resistance Faults

Learn why a wire or connection can appear normal during a basic continuity check but still cause problems when current flows.

Electrical diagnosis requires measurements under the correct operating conditions.

Why Continuity Testing Is Not Enough

A conductor or terminal can pass a basic continuity test and still fail when the circuit carries substantial current. Corrosion, loose crimps, damaged strands and poor ground connections can introduce resistance that causes voltage loss under load.

Understanding the Electrical Principle

Ohm's law describes the relationship between voltage, current and resistance:

V = I × R
Voltage = Current × Resistance

For example, a resistance of 0.05 ohms carrying 20 amperes produces a voltage drop of 1 volt. This is a mathematical example, not a universal pass-or-fail limit for every vehicle.

Understanding the Test

A voltage-drop test measures the voltage lost across a particular section of a circuit while that circuit is operating under an appropriate load.

  1. Confirm the reported symptom and identify the correct circuit.
  2. Consult the vehicle-specific wiring diagram.
  3. Inspect battery terminals, connectors, grounds and protective devices.
  4. Use a suitable multimeter and select the correct measurement setting.
  5. Operate the circuit under the specified test conditions.
  6. Measure across the section being investigated.
  7. Compare the reading with the manufacturer's specification.
  8. Repair any confirmed fault and repeat the test.

What Different Findings May Mean

ObservationPossible area to investigate
Excessive drop on the positive side Battery terminal, supply cable, fuse connection, relay contacts or other positive-side connections.
Excessive drop on the ground side Ground cable, body ground, engine ground strap or component ground connection.
Reading changes intermittently Loose terminal fit, damaged conductor, corrosion or an intermittent connection.
Passes without load but fails under load Possible high-resistance connection or conductor problem.
Important: Acceptable voltage-drop limits depend on the circuit, current, manufacturer and test conditions. Do not apply one generic number to every vehicle.

2. OBD-II Fault Codes, Freeze-Frame and Live Data

Understand what a diagnostic scanner can reveal and why reading a fault code is only the beginning of diagnosis.

Illustrative diagnostic equipment. Scanner functions vary by model and vehicle compatibility.

How Automotive Diagnostic Scanners Work

Modern vehicles use electronic control modules to monitor different systems. When a module detects a condition that meets its fault-recording criteria, it may store a diagnostic trouble code.

A code identifies a detected condition. It does not automatically identify the failed component. A sensor-related code, for example, may be caused by wiring damage, poor connections, incorrect supply voltage or a genuine sensor fault.

Recommended Diagnostic Sequence

  1. Identify the vehicle, engine and relevant control system.
  2. Confirm the customer's complaint.
  3. Scan the relevant control modules.
  4. Save the original fault report before clearing codes.
  5. Record stored, pending and permanent codes where supported.
  6. Review available freeze-frame information.
  7. Compare live data with manufacturer information and actual operating conditions.
  8. Perform the appropriate electrical or mechanical tests.
  9. Repair the confirmed cause and verify the result.

Understanding Scanner Information

InformationWhat it tells you
Stored DTC A fault recorded by a control module.
Pending DTC A detected condition that may not yet meet all confirmation requirements.
Freeze-frame May preserve engine speed, load, temperature and other conditions when a qualifying fault occurred.
Live data Current sensor or calculated values supported by the vehicle and scanner.
Readiness monitors Indicate whether supported emissions-monitor tests have completed.

Example: P0101 Mass-Airflow Performance Code

A P0101 code generally relates to mass-airflow range or performance. The exact diagnostic procedure depends on the vehicle. Replacing the mass-airflow sensor immediately may not solve the problem.

Possible areas to investigate include:

  • Air intake ducting and connections.
  • Air filter housing and incorrect assembly.
  • Sensor connector and wiring condition.
  • Sensor supply and ground where applicable.
  • Measured airflow compared with manufacturer data.
  • Other conditions that may affect engine airflow.
Professional practice: Preserve the original scan report. Clearing codes too early may remove useful diagnostic evidence or reset monitor status.

3. Automotive Sensor Diagnosis

Learn how to distinguish a faulty sensor from a wiring problem or an abnormal condition being measured.

Use the correct vehicle wiring diagram to identify sensor terminals and circuit functions.

Understanding Automotive Sensors

Sensors provide information that electronic control modules use to manage engine operation, vehicle speed, temperature, airflow, pressure and other functions.

Different sensors work in different ways. Some produce changing voltage signals, some use digital signals, and others operate through different electrical principles.

Three Important Parts of Sensor Diagnosis

  • Supply: Does the sensor receive the specified voltage or excitation?
  • Ground or return: Is the return circuit operating within the manufacturer's limits?
  • Signal: Does the output respond correctly to the physical condition being measured?

How to Approach a Sensor Fault

  1. Identify the sensor type and terminal functions from reliable service information.
  2. Inspect the connector, terminal fit and wiring.
  3. Check supply and ground using the approved procedure.
  4. Observe relevant live data where supported.
  5. Compare the signal with manufacturer specifications.
  6. Investigate wiring and related system conditions before condemning the sensor.

Why a Sensor Code Does Not Always Mean a Bad Sensor

A control module may report an implausible sensor signal because the sensor itself is defective. However, an open circuit, short circuit, poor ground, incorrect supply or mechanical problem may produce a similar result.

Safety: Do not apply battery voltage to an unknown sensor terminal or probe sensitive electronic circuits with unsuitable equipment. Incorrect testing can damage a control module.

4. Wiring Diagrams, Relays and Control Circuits

Understand how wiring diagrams help technicians trace power, ground and control signals through a vehicle.

Illustrative electrical schematic. Use the exact vehicle's wiring diagram for real diagnosis.

Why Wiring Diagrams Matter

A wiring diagram identifies circuit paths, connectors, splices, fuses, relays, grounds and control modules. It helps a technician understand how the electrical system is connected before testing begins.

Understanding Conventional Relays

A conventional electromechanical relay typically has a coil circuit and a switched contact circuit. The coil operates the contacts, allowing a control circuit to switch another electrical load.

  • The coil must receive the intended control conditions.
  • The contacts must switch the load supply as designed.
  • A relay may click while its contacts still have excessive resistance.
  • Some modern vehicles use electronic or solid-state switching instead.

Example: Cooling Fan Does Not Operate

A non-operating cooling fan can have several possible causes. The fault may involve the fan motor, supply, ground, fuse, relay, control circuit or operating conditions.

  1. Confirm the conditions under which the fan should operate.
  2. Check the relevant fuse and inspect the fan connector.
  3. Consult the wiring diagram to identify supply, ground and control paths.
  4. Test supply and ground using the approved procedure.
  5. Verify relay control and output if the design uses a relay.
  6. Use compatible scan-tool commanded operation if supported.
  7. Repair the confirmed fault and retest.
Safety warning: Electric cooling fans can start unexpectedly. Keep hands and tools clear. Do not bridge relay terminals or apply power to unknown control-module circuits without verified service information.

5. Starting and Charging System Diagnosis

Learn how battery, starter, alternator and cable faults can produce similar electrical symptoms.

Battery and charging-system complaints require testing before component replacement.

Understanding Starting-System Problems

The starting system uses the battery, cables, starter motor and associated control circuits to crank the engine. A slow-cranking complaint does not automatically mean the starter motor has failed.

For a Slow-Cranking Complaint

  1. Inspect battery terminals and cables.
  2. Test battery condition using the correct battery-test procedure.
  3. Measure battery voltage under specified cranking conditions.
  4. Perform positive-side and ground-side voltage-drop tests.
  5. If the battery and cables pass, investigate starter performance and mechanical causes.

Understanding Charging-System Problems

The charging system maintains battery charge and supplies electrical power while the engine is running. Depending on vehicle design, charging may be controlled electronically.

For a Charging Complaint

  1. Inspect the drive belt and connections where applicable.
  2. Check battery condition and relevant fault codes.
  3. Measure system voltage under manufacturer-specified conditions.
  4. Check charging current, control signals or alternator data where required.
  5. Investigate wiring, grounds, battery condition and charging control before replacing the alternator.

Modern vehicles may use smart charging systems that intentionally vary charging voltage according to battery state, temperature, electrical load and control strategy. A single universal voltage reading cannot diagnose every vehicle correctly.

Diagnostic record: Record actual battery-test results, cranking voltage, cable voltage drops and the verified repair. Do not publish measurements that were not actually taken.

6. Automotive Air-Conditioning Troubleshooting

Understand common car AC complaints, compressor operation, refrigerant leakage, airflow and electrical control problems.

Illustrative AC diagnostic equipment. Interpret readings using the correct refrigerant and vehicle service information.

Why a Car AC May Stop Cooling Properly

A vehicle air-conditioning system depends on several connected components. These include the compressor, condenser, evaporator, expansion device, refrigerant circuit, blower and electrical controls.

Weak cooling may result from poor condenser airflow, incorrect refrigerant charge, compressor-control issues, airflow problems, a blend-door fault or other system defects.

Step 1: Confirm the Complaint

Find out whether the AC problem occurs at idle, while driving or under both conditions. Check whether the blower operates and whether air comes out of the vents.

Step 2: Inspect Airflow

Poor airflow can make the cabin feel warm even when other parts of the refrigeration system are functioning. Possible areas include the cabin filter, blower motor, air distribution doors and evaporator airflow.

Step 3: Check Condenser Airflow

The condenser releases heat from the refrigerant. Restricted airflow or an improperly operating cooling fan can affect AC performance, particularly when the vehicle is stationary.

Step 4: Investigate Compressor Operation

Compressor designs vary. Some use conventional clutch operation while others use variable displacement or electronically controlled systems. Diagnosis must follow the particular compressor and vehicle design.

Step 5: Investigate Refrigerant Leakage

Refrigerant leakage can reduce cooling performance. Leak locations may include connections, hoses, seals, the condenser, evaporator or compressor-related fittings.

A proper leak investigation uses suitable equipment and an approved procedure. Refrigerant should not be released into the atmosphere.

Step 6: Interpret System Pressures

Pressure readings depend on refrigerant type, ambient temperature, engine speed, airflow and system operating conditions. A gauge reading should be interpreted alongside the manufacturer's specifications and other diagnostic findings.

Common AC Symptoms and Investigation Areas

SymptomAreas to investigate
Cooling improves while driving Condenser airflow, fan operation and performance at idle.
Blower works but air remains warm Compressor command, refrigerant circuit, blend door and control system.
Compressor does not engage Control request, wiring, pressure inputs, supply and compressor strategy.
Cooling is intermittent Intermittent electrical connections, sensor inputs, fan control and operating conditions.
Airflow is weak at vents Cabin filter, blower performance, evaporator airflow and air distribution.

Why Replacing the Compressor Is Not Always the Answer

A compressor-related complaint does not automatically prove the compressor itself has failed. Electrical controls, refrigerant conditions, system restrictions and other components may contribute to the symptom.

Refrigerant safety: Do not vent refrigerant or casually open a pressurised AC circuit. Refrigerant recovery, charging and leak testing require suitable equipment and training. Use the specified refrigerant and never mix refrigerants.

7. How to Document a Professional Diagnostic Case Study

Learn how to turn a completed diagnostic job into a useful technical report that explains the evidence behind a repair.

Show Readers How a Fault Was Confirmed

A good diagnostic case study explains the complaint, inspection, tests, findings, repair and final verification. It should help readers understand why a particular repair was recommended.

Recommended Case Study Structure

  1. Vehicle information: Make, model, year and engine where known.
  2. Customer complaint: The exact symptom and when it occurs.
  3. Initial inspection: Visible findings and scan results.
  4. Diagnostic plan: The system or circuit to investigate and why.
  5. Measurements: Actual readings, test conditions and reference specifications.
  6. Confirmed cause: The fault supported by the evidence.
  7. Repair: What was corrected and why.
  8. Verification: Repeat measurements or operating tests.

Example of Technical Reporting

A report might explain that an intermittent electrical fault was investigated, a ground connection was tested under load, and the measured result was compared with the applicable vehicle specification.

If a fault is confirmed, the report should explain what was corrected and how the system was checked afterwards. Only describe tests and repairs that were actually performed.

Using Photographs Properly

Original photographs can make a technical article more useful. Depending on the job, useful images may include:

  • The vehicle or component being investigated.
  • The location of the relevant component.
  • The test equipment used.
  • Actual meter readings or scan results.
  • The repaired area, where appropriate.

Explain what each image shows. Avoid displaying customer details or vehicle registration information without permission.

Important: Never invent readings, repairs, customer complaints or successful outcomes to make a case study appear more impressive.

8. Diagnostic Safety and Professional Practice

Good diagnosis also means protecting the technician, the vehicle and its electronic systems.

Important Safety Principles

Automotive electrical and diagnostic work involves electrical energy, moving components, hot surfaces, pressurised systems and sensitive electronic modules. The correct procedure depends on the vehicle and system.

Electrical Testing

  • Use service information for the exact vehicle.
  • Check meter settings, probe ratings and lead connections before testing.
  • Never measure resistance on an energised circuit.
  • Protect electronic circuits from accidental short circuits.
  • Use appropriate test equipment for sensitive control circuits.

Moving Engine Components

Fans, belts and other moving parts can operate unexpectedly. Keep hands, clothing and tools clear while the engine is running.

Airbag and Safety Systems

Airbag and restraint-system circuits require manufacturer- approved procedures. Do not probe or test these circuits using ordinary methods without the specified training and equipment.

Hybrid and Electric Vehicles

High-voltage systems in hybrid and electric vehicles require specific training, protective equipment and approved isolation procedures. Ordinary low-voltage automotive electrical experience is not a substitute for high-voltage qualification.

Air-Conditioning Systems

Refrigerant systems are pressurised. Use suitable recovery, charging and leak-detection equipment, and follow the correct handling procedures for the refrigerant used.

Remember: These guides provide general educational information. They do not replace manufacturer service manuals, vehicle-specific specifications, professional training or qualified diagnosis.
